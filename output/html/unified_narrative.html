How to read these lines

  • Contribution Profit = Gross Profit − Operating Expense. It deliberately excludes Acquisition Cost/CAC — CAC is spent once at booking but recovered over a customer's lifetime, so it's evaluated separately via LTV/CAC (Chapter 2), not netted into a single quarter's profit.
  • CAC = acquisition-family costs (marketing, origination, sign-on bonus, KYC/AML, added features, bounties) ÷ New Accounts, at the cohort's own booking quarter.
  • LTV = discounted cumulative Contribution Profit per account over a standardized 12-quarter (3-year) window at a 15% annual hurdle rate, so cohorts of very different ages compare on equal footing. See Chapter 2 for LTV by vintage and by FICO tier.

Chapter 2 · Cohort & Vintage Views

How the portfolio's unit economics evolve as it matures

Three lenses on the same book: how the balance mix ages, how a cohort's per-account economics develop over its own lifetime, and how profitable each vintage and each credit tier actually is.

The book is seasoning, not just growing

Young (0-4Q) Mid (5-8Q) Seasoned (9Q+)

Young-cohort share of the balance falls from 100% to 37%, while seasoned share rises from 0% to 39% over the forecast window — even as new cohorts keep originating, the existing book ages into "seasoned" faster than new originations can dilute it.

Per-account economics mature on a predictable curve

Merchant 1 shown, three representative vintages by age at booking. Every vintage starts negative (acquisition costs land before revenue does), climbs through its first several quarters, then plateaus — older and newer cohorts follow essentially the same shape at the same age, a sign the underlying unit economics are stable, not drifting. The newest vintage's later quarters are forecast, not yet observed.

LTV/CAC by vintage — is it improving as the book matures?

No strong secular trend either way: vintage-level LTV/CAC oscillates between 0.47x and 1.04x with no clear improving or worsening drift. Backbook vintages (booked on or before Q2 2026) average 0.77x; frontbook vintages booked during the forecast window average 0.86x — new cohorts are landing in the same range as historical ones, not a different regime.

LTV/CAC is inverted by FICO tier — sharply

Better credit tiers are less profitable to acquire, not more. High-FICO customers spend heavily — driving proportionally more rewards cost, since rewards scale with spend — but revolve less, generating far less interest income, the portfolio's largest revenue line. Lower-FICO customers carry higher charge-off rates, but a materially higher APR/yield on what they do revolve more than compensates. This is the single most consequential finding in this forecast (see Chapter 4).

Chapter 3 · The Working Model

Driver-based: raw cohort data in, forecast P&L out

Only 10 of the 34 raw line items are forecast as independent drivers (New Accounts, balances, transaction volume). Every recurring dollar line is historical rate × forecasted driver, with the rate itself a curve indexed by cohort age — not a flat number, so a genuinely flat rate (yield) and a genuinely curving one (loss rate) are handled by the same mechanism.

Backbook and frontbook, one engine

Existing cohorts (backbook) are anchored to their last actual value and rolled forward on the population's development-factor curve. New cohorts booked during the forecast window (frontbook) are seeded from a trailing-4-quarter New Accounts growth trend, capped at ±25%/quarter, then grown on the same curve. The only difference is where the starting size comes from.

Built and independently audited in parallel: 7/8 audit checks recompute every headline number via a separate code path from source — most starting from the raw source CSV, not the pipeline's own intermediate outputs — and pass. The one gap (a roll-forward identity check) is root-caused to new-cohort originations, not a modeling error. Full detail, including three real bugs a second independent review caught before these numbers shipped, is in How This Was Built.

The 3 biggest risks to this forecast

Risk 1 · Thin-history extrapolation

The two newest merchants have only 2–4 quarters of actual history. Their forecasts lean on pooled cross-merchant patterns rather than their own observed behavior.

Risk 2 · FICO-tier reward economics may be mispriced

The mechanism is real and traceable in the data, but this is a portfolio-wide average — worth validating against Imprint's own unit-economics view before acting on it.

Risk 3 · Every rate is held flat

Yield, loss rate, interchange, rewards — none carry a macro or competitive-response assumption into the 2-year horizon.

Where better data would help, in priority order

  1. Current, not just origination, FICO. The FICO-tier reveal above is the single most consequential finding in this forecast, built entirely on credit tier at booking. A refreshed/behavioral FICO feed would show whether it's really a FICO-tier effect or a credit-migration effect, and would sharpen every loss-rate curve at the same time.
  2. Fee Revenue and Other Credits lack a strong empirical driver. Best observed correlations are 0.47 (Fee Revenue vs. Outstanding Balance) and 0.19 (Other Credits, sporadic). Together under 5% of Gross Revenue, so low stakes today — but they're shipped on the best available proxy, not a validated relationship.
  3. More history for Merchants 9 & 10. 2 and 4 quarters of actuals respectively — both pooled to cross-merchant curves rather than forecast on their own observed behavior. A few more actual quarters would let each stand on its own.
  4. A macro or rate-environment signal. Every rate is held at its last observed level for 8 quarters; even a simple rate-environment or unemployment index would let a sensitivity range replace the current single point estimate.
Bottom line

Portfolio LTV/CAC: 0.85x

Below breakeven on a blended basis — driven almost entirely by the FICO-tier dynamic in Chapter 2, not by any single merchant. Several individual merchants and every below-Good FICO tier clear well above 1x; the blend is dragged down by acquisition spend on segments that don't pay it back within three years. That's a pricing/targeting question worth raising with the business, not a flaw in the forecast.
